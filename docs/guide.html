Ocean container routing, explained

An importer has 232 containers to move from three Chinese ports to two US ports over six weeks, on four carriers. This guide walks through the business problems that planning raises, shows each one as a scenario, and explains how the hosted Helixor Solver handles it. Every number is measured, and you can reproduce it from this repository.

1. The business problem

An importer buys goods from vendors in China and ships them to the US in containers. It doesn't own ships. It books space on sailings: one voyage of one carrier's service, leaving on a fixed day each week. In this example, four carriers run eight weekly services on three lanes (a lane is a port pair).

Four things make the booking hard:

Contracted space

Each carrier grants a weekly allocation on each sailing, for example 6 FEU. The importer has committed to it, so space it doesn't fill is money lost.

Spot space

Beyond the allocation a carrier may sell extra spot space at a higher rate, but only up to a limit, and only if it agrees.

Promise dates

Each container has a date its customer was promised. A sailing that arrives after it makes the container late.

Constant change

Sailings are blanked or delayed, cargo is late, vendors add orders, carriers cut allocations or refuse extra space. The plan has to be repaired without re-booking everything.

Sizes are counted in FEU (forty-foot equivalent units): a 40-foot container is 1 FEU, a 20-foot container is 0.5 FEU. The example has 155 40' high-cubes, 39 40' standard containers and 38 20' containers, and 224 FEU of allocation on the sailings of weeks 0 to 6.

2. What a good plan means

The business ranks its goals. The solver needs them as prices in one currency, so each goal becomes a weight per unit of shortfall. A higher goal gets a weight large enough that no amount of a lower goal can outweigh it in practice.

Why 100 for unused allocation and only 40 for spot? Allocation is paid for whether used or not, so leaving it empty and buying spot elsewhere is the worst of both. If your contracts work the other way, swap them; the weights are configuration, not code.

3. How the problem is modelled

The decision is simple to state: for each container, pick one sailing. It is bin packing (sailings are bins of limited space) combined with scheduling (each bin leaves and arrives on a fixed day).

All of this is declared, not programmed. The solver pack (pack/) is a zip of configuration: a manifest naming the decision (containers onto sailings), eight rules, and their weights. Each rule uses a generic contract, such as "an attribute of the container equals an attribute of the sailing" for the lane rule, or "the sum of FEU on a sailing stays below its capacity" for space. The solver has no ocean-shipping code. Your side does the lowering: it turns your records into the request, for example by working out, for each container, which sailings arrive on time.

5. Scenario: the first plan

Business

Solver

One request with every container and sailing, no prior plan. It searches for the cheapest assignment that breaks no hard rule.

6. Scenario: taking over another system's plan

Business

The importer already plans with another tool. It wants Helixor to take over from that plan, not start again.

Solver

The plan goes in with every booking frozen. The solver judges it under the pack's rules and moves nothing.

You see

The plan certified at the same penalty the scorer gives (42,780), or a refusal that names the broken rule and sailing.

Bring the plan as {"bookings": [{"container": …, "sailing": …}]} or {"placements": [{"entity": …, "group": …}]}. The example adopts the exact MIP's plan, standing in for the other system.

7. Scenario: the week goes wrong

Plans don't survive the week. Every few days the planner rolls the clock forward, applies what happened, and asks for a repair: a new plan that fixes what broke and moves as little else as possible. The example replays five cycles over the first three weeks.

One cycle, step by step

Which containers may move?

Moving a container means re-booking it with a carrier, so the repair request carries a pin for every container. The replan policy chooses them:

ContainerPinWhy
Locked by a plannerfrozenan explicit decision
Sailing's cutoff within 7 days, in confirmed spacefrozenalready booked with the carrier
Rolled off a sailingforbid that sailingthe carrier won't take it
Touched by an event (blank, delay, slip, expedite, new)none (free)must be re-planned
Everything elseprefer, change cost 25stays unless moving gains more than 25

A change cost of 25 means: move a container if that saves a late delivery (1,000) or fills a FEU of empty allocation (100), but not to shave four days off a container that is late anyway (4 × 5 = 20).

9. Scenario: asking carriers for more space

The allocation is a contract, so the carrier has already agreed to it. Anything beyond it is a request. The importer can over-allocate, asking for more than the allocation and seeing if the carrier accepts, often through the carrier's booking website. The carrier may say no. And when space is freed up, a planner doesn't necessarily hand it back, because a later change may need it.

Business

A booking on unconfirmed space isn't really booked. If the carrier says no, those containers must move.

Solver

Each sailing carries confirmed_feu and committed_feu (confirmed plus pending). After every plan, the planner works out requests, holds and releases. A rejection lowers the sailing's capacity, and the next repair moves what no longer fits.

You see

Lines like carrier action: request 2 FEU on ATLS-PSX-W05 and BORS-TP1-W03: carrier REJECTED 1 FEU; space now capped at 5 FEU.

Two details follow from the business rule. The replan policy freezes a booking near cutoff only within confirmed space, because a booking on pending space isn't with the carrier yet; the run says so in a note. And in the replay, a simulator plays the carriers: the scenario's hidden simulation block says how much extra each carrier will really take. The planner never reads it and it is never sent to the solver; a test checks this. Plans are made on what carriers have said, not on what they will say.

In the replay, after cycle 1 the plan holds 0.5 FEU of confirmed space it no longer needs on BORS-TP2-W02. It keeps it through cycle 2 in case a later change needs it, and releases it in cycle 3, once that sailing's cutoff is within two days.

10. Scenario: a peak-season surge

Business

On day 17 a vendor adds 10 FEU for Shanghai to Los Angeles. The lane's weekly allocation is 14 FEU and already nearly full. The demand can't fit in the contract.

Solver

It fills the allocation and buys spot for the rest, choosing sailings that keep the most containers on time. The planner requests the extra space.

You see

Spot rises from 18.5 to 28.5 FEU with 99.6% of the allocation used; then carriers reject 6 of 12 FEU, and the next repair re-routes.

11. How good are the plans?

An exact MIP gets the exact same request as the Helixor solver and proves the optimum. A from-scratch hosted solve shows what re-planning everything would move.

CycleHelixor repairOptimum, same pinsFrom scratch
1 · day 345,160 · 16 moved45,160 · 1345,220 · 72
2 · day 850,040 · 1450,005 · 1348,790 · 72
3 · day 1546,825 · 546,825 · 243,705 · 60
4 · day 17 (surge)54,825 · 354,825 · 346,890 · 54
5 · day 1953,740 · 653,740 · 645,815 · 77

Penalty excludes the change cost; "moved" counts containers whose sailing changed from the previous plan.

  • Every repair is feasible, confirmed by the independent scorer.
  • The Helixor repair reaches the proven same-pins optimum in cycles 1, 3, 4 and 5, and is 0.07% above it in cycle 2 (35 points), in 2.1–4.1 seconds.
  • Repair moves far less than re-planning: 3–16 containers against 54–77. Where it moves a few more than the optimum, the extra moves are free containers (ones an event touched), which carry no change cost, so they leave the score unchanged.
  • From scratch scores lower from cycle 2 on because nothing holds it to the bookings already with carriers. It reaches that by moving 54–77 containers, frozen bookings included, which is why it isn't a plan anyone would send.
Measured on the next solver release. These figures come from the next Helixor solver release, run on a local server with the same requests; the hosted service gets it on its next deploy. That release closed a gap the hosted repair had in these cycles (4.5–10% above the same-pins optimum) with two generic search changes: each scoring level now judges a worse move in its own units, and every solve ends with a pass that proves no single move or swap improves the plan. Neither change knows about containers or sailings.

Why a declared model can be proven optimal

The exact MIP in this example (ocean_routing/optimum.py) is not a second model of the business. It restates the manifest's own rules as equations, rule for rule, and it proves the optimum of every cycle in 0.02–0.3 seconds. That is possible because each rule in the manifest has a declared shape, and every one of those shapes can be written as linear equations:

RuleDeclared shapeAs equations
lane match, cargo ready by cutoffwhich sailings a container may usethe container's choice is restricted to those sailings
sailing capacitya sum over a sailing, at most a limitone inequality per sailing
on-time delivery, days latea cost for each container–sailing paira coefficient on that choice
allocation, spot, unconfirmed and new spacea cost of a sailing's load above or below a lineone extra variable and one inequality per line

What follows from the model being declared rather than programmed:

  • A proven answer where the rules allow one. When every rule converts, the answer can come with a proof that no better plan exists, not just a good plan.
  • A measured gap where they don't. Converting the rules that do convert gives a bound, so a plan found by search can say how far from the best possible it can be.
  • One model to maintain. The equations come from the manifest, so a change to a rule, a weight or a customer's configuration reaches the exact method and the search at once. Nobody keeps a second copy in step.
  • Every answer stays checkable. Whichever method produced the plan, the certificate scores it against the same declared rules, and the independent scorer re-checks it.
Status. In this example the MIP is written by hand from the manifest, as a yardstick. A Solver answer says for itself whether it is proven optimal: the demo-week solve in section 12 came back with proven_optimal: true. Read proven_optimal in each answer rather than assume it.

12. Calling the Solver API

Every hosted command in this repository makes the same few REST calls, and ocean_routing/client.py is the shortest complete client. This section documents those calls for this problem type: what to send, what comes back, and how to act on it. Every request and response below was run against a Helixor Solver server with pack 2.4 published (sha256 fe84dcc6…8e34418), and the values shown are the ones it returned. That server ran locally with sign-in switched off, so $SOLVER_TOKEN stands for the token the hosted service checks.

Before the first solve: publish and select the pack

The model is not in the request. Publishing the pack makes its problem type, ocean_container_routing, available to your organization, and every solve of that type runs the active version of the pack. Nothing in the solve body picks a manifest. A request that carries its own options.manifest is refused by a default deployment (422 unsupported_problem_type), so a solve runs only rules that were validated and admitted with a pack.

CallWhat it does
POST /api/v1/solver-packs/validations?expected_sha256=…Validates the zip (body application/zip) without storing it. The demo pack answers ADMITTED, 0 errors, 0 warnings.
POST /api/v1/solver-packs?activate=true&expected_sha256=…Stores the version and makes it active. A version is immutable. Publishing 2.4 again with different bytes is refused with 409 solver_pack_version_conflict, which names both hashes. Earlier server releases answer 422 solver_pack_rejected. Either way, any change needs a new version.
GET /api/v1/solver-packs/ocean_container_routing/versionsEvery stored version, with active: the pack_id, version and sha256 that solves run.
GET /api/v1/solver-packs/ocean_container_routing/historyThe pack's append-only log: one event per change, with kind (published, activated, superseded, retired or deleted), version, sha256, actor, at and the version it replaced.

FieldMeaning
problem.containers[]The entities. The manifest's data_schema requires id, lane, feu, ready_day, promise_day and on_time_sailings (the ids of the sailings on the container's lane that arrive by promise_day; your lowering derives this list). The rule views also read size.
problem.sailings[]The groups. Required: id, lane, cutoff_day, eta_day, allocation_feu, capacity_feu (allocation plus the spot space on sale), confirmed_feu (space the carrier has acknowledged) and committed_feu (acknowledged plus requested). The views also read carrier, week and etd_day.
problem.prior_planRepair only: the previous plan, as {"container id": "sailing id"}.
problem.pins[]Repair only: {"entity": id, "mode": "frozen" | "forbid" | "prefer", "group": sailing id}. frozen keeps the container on group. forbid keeps it off group. prefer keeps it on its prior sailing unless moving pays more than change_cost.
problem.pin_defaultRepair only: the mode for every container that has no pin of its own, for example "prefer".
problem.change_costRepair only: the price of moving a prefer container, in the same units as the rule weights (manifest default 25).
solver.modeExecution mode. "local" runs the solve on the Solver service itself, and that is the mode this example uses. Asking for a GPU or cloud backend or device needs the solver.gpu entitlement. Without it the answer is 403 remote_solve_not_entitled, with a repair_hint telling you to drop that option. Pinned solves always run on the service.
optionsSend {} for this pack: the search budget comes from the manifest's search: block. options.constraints is an object of {"rule id": {parameter overrides}} (not a list). It can switch on a rule that a manifest leaves off and override a rule's parameters. It cannot switch a rule off: {"minimize_spot": {"enabled": false}} is accepted and ignored, and the penalty stays 1,550. Every rule in this pack is on by default. A list here currently fails with a plain 500. Budget keys are checked against the service's ceilings: time_limit_seconds: 9999 is refused with 422 input_limit_exceeded, and time_limit_seconds: 1 returned the same plan. Keys that start with __ belong to the service and are refused with 422 reserved_option_key.

When the solve fails

The service fails closed. It refuses a request it cannot solve rather than answer something else, and a 422 fails the same way every time you repeat it. Failure bodies come in three shapes, so read the code from the right place:

  • Solve failures (missing_problem_data, input_limit_exceeded, reserved_option_key, unsupported_problem_type, remote_solve_not_entitled, solver_pack_version_conflict) carry failure_code and detail at the top level. Input faults also carry status: "unsupported", problem_type, missing_inputs, error_class, http_status, job_id and a verdict with status: "failed" and an inputs_hash.
  • Request and caller checks (invalid_solve_id, invalid_token, and the run lookups) nest them one level down: {"detail": {"failure_code": …, "detail": …}}.
  • A body of the wrong shape, such as "solver": "local", is a 422 whose detail is a list of field errors with no failure_code. An unknown job is a 404 whose detail is a sentence.

One gap: options.constraints sent as a list currently returns a plain 500 instead of a typed refusal. Send it as an object.

Using the answer

An HTTP 200 alone is never enough. The example's planner refuses an answer that is not feasible, that leaves a container unbooked, or that the independent scorer rejects. Against the raw response, a full acceptance check also covers the penalty bands, the rule evaluation and the pack's provenance:

body = response.json()                      # after checking response.status_code == 200
sol, adm = body["solution"], body["solution"]["admission"]
pack = body["result"]["metadata"]["solver_pack"]
accepted = (
    body["status"] == "completed"
    and sol["is_feasible"] and adm["is_feasible"]
    and adm["eligibility_penalty"] == 0 and adm["hard_penalty"] == 0
    and not adm["unplaced_entities"]
    and sol["entities_placed"] == sol["entities_total"]
    and not sol["unevaluable_rules"]
    and (pack["version"], pack["sha256"]) == ("2.4", PUBLISHED_SHA256)
)
if not accepted:
    raise SystemExit(adm["reason"])         # keep the previous plan; never pass this one on

by_container = {p["entity"]: p["group"] for p in sol["placements"]}
by_sailing = {}
for container, sailing in by_container.items():
    by_sailing.setdefault(sailing, []).append(container)

Then, from your own records:

  • Per container: by_container is the booking to make. CNSHA-0028 goes on ATLS-PSX-W02. In a repair, pins.placement_provenance tells you which bookings are new (moved) and which stand.
  • Per sailing: add up the FEU on each sailing and compare it with that sailing's allocation_feu, confirmed_feu and capacity_feu. FEU above the allocation is spot, and FEU above committed_feu is space still to request from the carrier. In the demo week, ATLS-PSX-W02 carries 9 FEU on a 6 FEU allocation, and ATLS-PSX-W03 and BORS-TP2-W02 carry 1 FEU over theirs: 5 FEU of spot in total, all still to request. ATLS-PSX-W01 and COBT-CPX-W02 carry nothing, and BORS-TP2-W01 carries 2.5 FEU of its 5.
  • Which sailings a rule names: map affected_groups through group_ids. That turns minimize_spot's [1, 2, 10] into the three sailings above, which are the carriers to ask for space.
  • KPIs and views: the pack declares them in the manifest's ui.visualizations (GET /api/v1/manifests/ocean_container_routing), not in the solve response. Each KPI names a rule and a measure, and you evaluate it from your records and the placements, the way ocean_routing/report.py and the pack's solution surface do. For the demo week they come out as: on time 100%, late containers 0, allocation used 73.9% (32.5 of 44 FEU), spot space 5 FEU, days late 0, on unacknowledged space 5 FEU, space still to request 5 FEU. These agree with the certificate: 11.5 FEU of allocation unused, and 5 FEU on minimize_spot. The other declared views, load by sailing, allocation used by week and carrier, the sailing schedule and late containers, bind to the same records and placements.
  • Re-check it yourself: python -m ocean_routing score scores any plan from the records alone, without the solver.
Never read KPIs off an infeasible plan. In the no-spot week the views count the two unplaced containers as late (5 late, against 3 in on_time_delivery), and the plan cannot be booked anyway. Check feasibility first.

Handling failures. A 422 is a fault in the request: fix the input and send it again, because retrying unchanged repeats the failure. A 403 names the entitlement or option to drop. If the connection drops during a solve, don't start another solve straight away. Send every solve with an X-Helixor-Solve-Id, then read GET /api/v1/solve/runs/{id} for its job_id and fetch the answer with GET /api/v1/solve/{job_id}. Both are kept in the memory of the server instance that ran the solve. A finished run is dropped after 10 minutes, and behind several instances or after a restart it may not be found at all. Either way the lookup answers 404, and then you solve again. A stop request can answer 409 solve_not_stoppable when the solve is running where a stop cannot reach it. Set your client timeout above the solve's budget: the generated client below defaults to 120 seconds. A repair should keep the previous plan until a new one passes the acceptance check.

13. A typed client, generated from the contract

This repository calls the API with plain HTTP, so it stays public and needs no private package. Helixor also provides typed clients for its APIs. They are generated by Helixor's own SDK generator, helixor-sdk-gen, from each service's OpenAPI contract. For the Solver, the client is the Python package helixor-solver-contract. It covers solve, job status, pre-solve checks, recommendations, health and version.

How the client is produced

  1. The contract comes from the server. The Solver server dumps the solve-contract part of its OpenAPI from the same models that validate requests. Before writing this section we dumped the contract from the current server and regenerated the client from it. The solve request, solve response and failure envelope were identical to the published release. Regenerating from the release's own contract reproduced the published client byte for byte, and its tests passed. The only difference from today's dump was the web framework's generic validation-error schema, which no solve call reads.
  2. The generator turns the contract into a client. It produces typed request and response models, one method per operation, bearer authentication, typed exceptions, and the runtime policy set in the project's configuration (timeouts and retries).
  3. The output is checked, then released. The generated models must parse payloads captured from the server application, and the client is regenerated from the committed contract and compared file by file before a release. Releases are tagged, for example helixor-solver-contract-sdk/v0.1.0. The same generator produces the clients for Helixor's other APIs, in Python, TypeScript and Java.

Installing it

The package is distributed from a private Helixor repository under a proprietary license. It is not on a public package index, so ask Helixor for access. Without access, use raw REST, which needs nothing private.

Both columns use one small helper, because failure codes sit at the top level or one level down (see When the solve fails):

def failure_code(body):
    if isinstance(body, dict):
        if "failure_code" in body:
            return body["failure_code"]
        if isinstance(body.get("detail"), dict):
            return body["detail"].get("failure_code")
    return None                    # a field-error list or a sentence

Both return the same plan, and in both solution is a JSON object. The SDK types the response envelope, but the plan the manifest engine returns inside solution and result is open, because its fields depend on the pack. So the acceptance check in Using the answer reads the same in both, and so does the failure handling: exc.parsed_error is the same failure body you would parse from resp.json(), in the same three shapes.

14. Running the solver locally: the embedded runtime

Everything above calls a Solver service over the network. The Helixor Decision Runtime (the proprietary helixor-runtime package; ask Helixor for access) can instead run Helixor's solver inside your own process. In that mode there is no network call, the records never leave your host, and the answer comes back as a function result. This section shows how to switch it on, what it needs, and what it can run today. Every result here comes from runtime code current on 2026-11-08, run on this example's demo week.

One explicit switch

import json
from pii_guard.license import LicenseVerifier
from helixor_runtime import HelixorSolver, SolverOptionError

# Embedded: helixor-solvers runs in this process and never calls a service.
lic = LicenseVerifier.load("helixor.hxlic")             # verifies the signature
solver = HelixorSolver(mode="embedded", license=lic)

# Hosted: the same calls, delegated to a Solver service through its generated client.
hosted = HelixorSolver(mode="hosted", base_url=SOLVER_URL, auth=SOLVER_TOKEN)

The mode has no default, and neither mode falls back to the other. HelixorSolver(mode="") raises SolverConfigurationError. A missing engine or an unreachable service is also a typed error, never a quiet switch to the other mode. Both modes expose solve, feasibility and recommendations, return the same solve contract (verdict, the plan only when it is feasible, named shortfalls, ranked alternates, a certificate, and metadata.model saying which mode answered), and raise the same typed errors. Install the extras you need: solvers for embedded routing, solvers-full for embedded rostering, and solvers-hosted for the hosted mode. The embedded engine, helixor-solvers, is a private Helixor package as well.

The license decides

The embedded mode solves only under a signed Helixor license whose licensed_solvers cover the solver, and the runtime checks this on every solve. The demo licenses used here give these results:

LicenseEmbedded routing solve

The hosted mode takes no license: the service authorizes each call by your token, as in section 12.

What it can run today, and what it can't

The runtime's solve contract covers two problem types: vrp (vehicle routing) and rostering. This example's problem type is a solver pack, and the embedded mode does not run solver packs yet. It refuses the type, by name, rather than answering something else:

problem = json.load(open("pack/datasets/two_lane_week.demo.json"))
try:
    solver.solve("ocean_container_routing", problem)
except SolverOptionError as exc:
    print(exc.failure_code, "|", exc)
# unsupported_problem_type | no solve contract for problem type
#   'ocean_container_routing'; supported: ['rostering', 'vrp']

The runtime's hosted mode can't serve this pack either. It expects a solve-contract answer, and a pack solve returns the plan and certificate documented in section 12 instead. It raises SolverServiceError ("answered … without a solve-contract answer"). For this problem, call the Solver API directly, over raw REST or the generated client (section 13).

Running this example on your own machine

To run the container routing pack locally today, you need the Solver server running on your own host. It is a private Helixor package, so ask Helixor for it, then point SOLVER_URL at it instead of the hosted service. Then publish the pack to it (python -m ocean_routing publish-pack), and every command and example in sections 12 and 13 runs unchanged. That is how this guide's examples were produced: the server ran on the same machine as the client, and the demo week solved in about 30 ms once warm. The first solve on a freshly started server took about 45 seconds. The Solver server is not part of this repository.
