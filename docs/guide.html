Ocean container routing, explained

An importer has 232 containers to move from three Chinese ports to two US ports over six weeks, on four carriers. This guide walks through the business problems that planning raises, shows each one as a scenario, and explains how the hosted Helixor Solver handles it. Every number is measured, and you can reproduce it from this repository.

1. The business problem

An importer buys goods from vendors in China and ships them to the US in containers. It doesn't own ships. It books space on sailings: one voyage of one carrier's service, leaving on a fixed day each week. In this example, four carriers run eight weekly services on three lanes (a lane is a port pair).

Four things make the booking hard:

Contracted space

Each carrier grants a weekly allocation on each sailing, for example 6 FEU. The importer has committed to it, so space it doesn't fill is money lost.

Spot space

Beyond the allocation a carrier may sell extra spot space at a higher rate, but only up to a limit, and only if it agrees.

Promise dates

Each container has a date its customer was promised. A sailing that arrives after it makes the container late.

Constant change

Sailings are blanked or delayed, cargo is late, vendors add orders, carriers cut allocations or refuse extra space. The plan has to be repaired without re-booking everything.

Sizes are counted in FEU (forty-foot equivalent units): a 40-foot container is 1 FEU, a 20-foot container is 0.5 FEU. The example has 155 40' high-cubes, 39 40' standard containers and 38 20' containers, and 224 FEU of allocation on the sailings of weeks 0 to 6.

2. What a good plan means

The business ranks its goals. The solver needs them as prices in one currency, so each goal becomes a weight per unit of shortfall. A higher goal gets a weight large enough that no amount of a lower goal can outweigh it in practice.

Why 100 for unused allocation and only 40 for spot? Allocation is paid for whether used or not, so leaving it empty and buying spot elsewhere is the worst of both. If your contracts work the other way, swap them; the weights are configuration, not code.

3. How the problem is modelled

The decision is simple to state: for each container, pick one sailing. It is bin packing (sailings are bins of limited space) combined with scheduling (each bin leaves and arrives on a fixed day).

All of this is declared, not programmed. The solver pack (pack/) is a zip of configuration: a manifest naming the decision (containers onto sailings), eight rules, and their weights. Each rule uses a generic contract, such as "an attribute of the container equals an attribute of the sailing" for the lane rule, or "the sum of FEU on a sailing stays below its capacity" for space. The solver has no ocean-shipping code. Your side does the lowering: it turns your records into the request, for example by working out, for each container, which sailings arrive on time.

5. Scenario: the first plan

Business

It's Monday of week 0. All 232 containers need a sailing for the next six weeks.

Solver

One request with every container and sailing, no prior plan. It searches for the cheapest assignment that breaks no hard rule.

6. Scenario: taking over another system's plan

Business

The importer already plans with another tool. It wants Helixor to take over from that plan, not start again.

Solver

The plan goes in with every booking frozen. The solver judges it under the pack's rules and moves nothing.

You see

The plan certified at the same penalty the scorer gives (42,780), or a refusal that names the broken rule and sailing.

Bring the plan as {"bookings": [{"container": …, "sailing": …}]} or {"placements": [{"entity": …, "group": …}]}. The example adopts the exact MIP's plan, standing in for the other system.

7. Scenario: the week goes wrong

Plans don't survive the week. Every few days the planner rolls the clock forward, applies what happened, and asks for a repair: a new plan that fixes what broke and moves as little else as possible. The example replays five cycles over the first three weeks.

One cycle, step by step

Which containers may move?

Moving a container means re-booking it with a carrier, so the repair request carries a pin for every container. The replan policy chooses them:

ContainerPinWhy
Locked by a plannerfrozenan explicit decision
Sailing's cutoff within 7 days, in confirmed spacefrozenalready booked with the carrier
Rolled off a sailingforbid that sailingthe carrier won't take it
Touched by an event (blank, delay, slip, expedite, new)none (free)must be re-planned
Everything elseprefer, change cost 25stays unless moving gains more than 25

A change cost of 25 means: move a container if that saves a late delivery (1,000) or fills a FEU of empty allocation (100), but not to shave four days off a container that is late anyway (4 × 5 = 20).

9. Scenario: asking carriers for more space

The allocation is a contract, so the carrier has already agreed to it. Anything beyond it is a request. The importer can over-allocate, asking for more than the allocation and seeing if the carrier accepts, often through the carrier's booking website. The carrier may say no. And when space is freed up, a planner doesn't necessarily hand it back, because a later change may need it.

Business

A booking on unconfirmed space isn't really booked. If the carrier says no, those containers must move.

Solver

Each sailing carries confirmed_feu and committed_feu (confirmed plus pending). After every plan, the planner works out requests, holds and releases. A rejection lowers the sailing's capacity, and the next repair moves what no longer fits.

You see

Lines like carrier action: request 2 FEU on ATLS-PSX-W05 and BORS-TP1-W03: carrier REJECTED 1 FEU; space now capped at 5 FEU.

Two details follow from the business rule. The replan policy freezes a booking near cutoff only within confirmed space, because a booking on pending space isn't with the carrier yet; the run says so in a note. And in the replay, a simulator plays the carriers: the scenario's hidden simulation block says how much extra each carrier will really take. The planner never reads it and it is never sent to the solver; a test checks this. Plans are made on what carriers have said, not on what they will say.

In the replay, after cycle 1 the plan holds 0.5 FEU of confirmed space it no longer needs on BORS-TP2-W02. It keeps it through cycle 2 in case a later change needs it, and releases it in cycle 3, once that sailing's cutoff is within two days.

10. Scenario: a peak-season surge

Business

On day 17 a vendor adds 10 FEU for Shanghai to Los Angeles. The lane's weekly allocation is 14 FEU and already nearly full. The demand can't fit in the contract.

Solver

It fills the allocation and buys spot for the rest, choosing sailings that keep the most containers on time. The planner requests the extra space.

You see

Spot rises from 18.5 to 28.5 FEU with 99.6% of the allocation used; then carriers reject 6 of 12 FEU, and the next repair re-routes.

11. How good are the plans?

An exact MIP gets the exact same request as the Helixor solver and proves the optimum. A from-scratch hosted solve shows what re-planning everything would move.

CycleHelixor repairOptimum, same pinsFrom scratch
1 · day 345,160 · 16 moved45,160 · 1345,220 · 72
2 · day 850,040 · 1450,005 · 1348,790 · 72
3 · day 1546,825 · 546,825 · 243,705 · 60
4 · day 17 (surge)54,825 · 354,825 · 346,890 · 54
5 · day 1953,740 · 653,740 · 645,815 · 77

Penalty excludes the change cost; "moved" counts containers whose sailing changed from the previous plan.

  • Every repair is feasible, confirmed by the independent scorer.
  • The Helixor repair reaches the proven same-pins optimum in cycles 1, 3, 4 and 5, and is 0.07% above it in cycle 2 (35 points), in 2.1–4.1 seconds.
  • Repair moves far less than re-planning: 3–16 containers against 54–77. Where it moves a few more than the optimum, the extra moves are free containers (ones an event touched), which carry no change cost, so they leave the score unchanged.
  • From scratch scores lower from cycle 2 on because nothing holds it to the bookings already with carriers. It reaches that by moving 54–77 containers, frozen bookings included, which is why it isn't a plan anyone would send.
Measured on the next solver release. These figures come from the next Helixor solver release, run on a local server with the same requests; the hosted service gets it on its next deploy. That release closed a gap the hosted repair had in these cycles (4.5–10% above the same-pins optimum) with two generic search changes: each scoring level now judges a worse move in its own units, and every solve ends with a pass that proves no single move or swap improves the plan. Neither change knows about containers or sailings.

Why a declared model can be proven optimal

The exact MIP in this example (ocean_routing/optimum.py) is not a second model of the business. It restates the manifest's own rules as equations, rule for rule, and it proves the optimum of every cycle in 0.02–0.3 seconds. That is possible because each rule in the manifest has a declared shape, and every one of those shapes can be written as linear equations:

RuleDeclared shapeAs equations
lane match, cargo ready by cutoffwhich sailings a container may usethe container's choice is restricted to those sailings
sailing capacitya sum over a sailing, at most a limitone inequality per sailing
on-time delivery, days latea cost for each container–sailing paira coefficient on that choice
allocation, spot, unconfirmed and new spacea cost of a sailing's load above or below a lineone extra variable and one inequality per line

What follows from the model being declared rather than programmed:

  • A proven answer where the rules allow one. When every rule converts, the answer can come with a proof that no better plan exists, not just a good plan.
  • A measured gap where they don't. Converting the rules that do convert gives a bound, so a plan found by search can say how far from the best possible it can be.
  • One model to maintain. The equations come from the manifest, so a change to a rule, a weight or a customer's configuration reaches the exact method and the search at once. Nobody keeps a second copy in step.
  • Every answer stays checkable. Whichever method produced the plan, the certificate scores it against the same declared rules, and the independent scorer re-checks it.
Status. In this example the MIP is written by hand from the manifest, as a yardstick. Generating it from the manifest, and choosing between exact solving and search per request, is planned and not yet part of the hosted solver. Rules about sequences and timing along a route, and very large instances, will stay with search.

13. Glossary

FEUForty-foot equivalent unit. A 40' container is 1 FEU, a 20' container 0.5.
LaneAn origin–destination port pair, such as CNSHA-USLAX.
SailingOne weekly voyage of one carrier service, for example ATLS-PSX-W03: Atlas Line, service PSX, week 3.
CutoffThe last day the container yard accepts cargo for a sailing.
AllocationWeekly space a carrier has contracted to the importer on a sailing. Already acknowledged.
SpotSpace beyond the allocation, bought at market rates, up to what the carrier has on sale.
Promise dateThe day the container's customer was promised delivery.
Solver packA versioned zip of configuration (manifest, rules, weights, a demo dataset) published to the hosted solver. Versions are immutable.
LoweringTurning your records into the request: deriving on-time sailings, capacity, confirmed and committed space.
CertificateThe solver's account of a plan: feasible or not, and each rule's penalty.
PinPer-container instruction in a repair: frozen, forbid, prefer, or none.
Change costThe price of moving a prefer container, in the same units as the weights.
Confirmed / committedConfirmed: space the carrier acknowledged. Committed: confirmed plus pending requests.
